Pros and
Pros

On decisions, debt, and the lie that one path must be better than another.

Most of us were taught to make decisions the same way: list the pros, list the cons, weigh them carefully, and call the result "rational." But no one ever taught us the math. Do we subtract the cons from the pros? Are all pros and cons equal? How much weight does each carry? When new information appears, do we rerun the equation? And maybe the biggest question of all — how do we know we listed the right inputs to begin with?

Like most of our mental habits, the pro-and-con list arrives already accepted as truth. Few of us stop to ask whether there's another way to think about a decision. Fewer still stop to ask whether the struggle is coming not from the decision itself, but from the assumptions we quietly built into it.

Coming out of law school, I applied for a one-year clerkship in the Southern District of New York — the kind of opportunity people spend years angling for. Prestigious. A front-row seat to how decisions actually get made. The kind of line that shows how hard you work and how smart you are. What came back was an offer for two years, not one.

I was beyond excited, and immediately unsettled. Two years meant twice the mentorship, twice the prestige. It also meant an extra year before I could start the corporate work I had discovered I wanted to pursue, and an extra year before I could start paying down the debt that was quietly accruing interest in the background of every decision I made that year.

For days I ran the same loop. If I accepted, I would have to delay debt repayment and be behind another year of experience in the field I wanted to pursue. If I declined, I lost a kind of experience you can't manufacture later — the mentorship, the prestige, a door that might not open twice. I wasn't deciding. I was grieving two futures at once, in advance, before either had even happened.

Looking back, I wasn't really weighing two options. I was weighing two fears. I struggled because I didn't want to make a mistake. Voices in my head warning me about what I might be missing out on if I didn't choose correctly. Neither voice was helping me see the decision clearly. Both were just asking me to avoid whichever future felt scarier.

But what if I had been weighing the wrong things?

Somewhere in the middle of that loop, the framing cracked open. The clerkship was genuinely great. And no clerkship — corporate work, paying down debt — was also genuinely great. I'd been asking each opportunity to justify itself against what it would cost me instead of asking whether it was good in its own right. Once I put just the pros on the same scale — nothing else, no cons on either side — there was no wrong side left to land on.

Pros and pros. Two good outcomes — not a search for the choice I wouldn't regret, but for the one that felt most aligned. Nothing on either side was a loss, only a different shape of good. And if the two sides had weighed exactly the same, that would have been fine too. I could have flipped a coin.

Both were genuinely great. It wouldn't have mattered which one I chose.

Once I saw that, the torment didn't ease gradually. It just stopped. I called the judge's chambers and politely declined. Not anxiously. Not defensively. Just clearly — the way you decline something when you already know what you're choosing toward instead of what you're running from.

The judge offered me the one-year clerkship instead.

I don't tell that part to suggest life rewards clear decisions with the outcome you secretly wanted. Sometimes it doesn't, and that has to be fine too. I tell it because of what changed before I ever picked up the phone — the part that had nothing to do with the outcome and everything to do with how I was standing inside the decision.

Here's the idea worth sitting with: there's the narrative self, the one running the loop — what am I giving up, what will I regret, what if I choose wrong — and there's the observing self, the one quietly watching the loop run and asking whether any of it is even true. The narrative self treats every option as a door closing somewhere. The observing self appreciates the doors that close, then walks through the one it chose.

This isn't the same as pretending the harder path doesn't have real costs. The two-year clerkship really would have delayed the debt payoff. That wasn't fear talking — that was prudence, and prudence deserves a seat at the table. But prudence and fear often wear the same clothes. Prudence says, here's what's actually true about each path. Fear says, if you choose this, you'll lose something you'll never recover. One helps you see reality. The other asks you to organize your life around avoiding regret. Those are not the same thing.

Zest without prudence is recklessness. Prudence without zest is just fear with better posture.

Following what's aligned isn't sticking your head in the sand. It doesn't mean ignoring debt, or risk, or the parts of a decision that are genuinely hard. It means looking honestly at the facts, acknowledging the fears they awaken, and refusing to let those fears masquerade as objective truth.

The next time a decision feels impossible, ask yourself a different question: are you choosing between two futures? Or between two fears? Because once both possibilities are allowed to be genuinely good, something unexpected happens. The decision not only becomes obvious, it becomes peaceful.
